    Klang Valley
    Flood Watch

    A live map of flood telemetry for Selangor, Kuala Lumpur and Putrajaya. It draws water levels, rainfall, warning sirens and flood gauges, from every station the Department of Irrigation and Drainage publishes. The map refreshes itself.

    Each official portal holds one part of that data. None of them draws the parts together. This site joins the three feeds, works out which stations climb toward their own danger marks, and states that instead.

    Not an official warning channel. This site is not affiliated with JPS. It re-publishes public data and can be wrong, stale or down. In an emergency, follow JPS and NADMA directly.
    If you are in danger, call 999, or the flood emergency line for your state: civildefence.gov.my (APM).
    JPS publishes its own flood statements at publicinfobanjir.water.gov.my.

    Origin

    This site started with one question. Is the river near my house rising? The answer needed three government websites, so I joined them into one page.

    An AI wrote most of the code, and these paragraphs. The work took a few evenings. I reviewed each change and stated what was wrong with it.

    The first version covered Selangor alone. Kuala Lumpur went in next, because I work there every day. Putrajaya arrived on the same feed. The repository still carries the old name.

    The cameras took the longest. JPS publishes a coordinate for every flood camera, and one batch of them came out shuffled. A camera named after a town in Kuala Selangor drew its pin 83 km away, in Bangi. No second source can settle it, because the list and the detail carry the same wrong number.

    So I checked those cameras by hand. I stood in front of some of them. For the rest I read the picture: a road sign, a bridge, the bend of a river. Then I matched that view against a map until the place and the station name agreed.

    Warranty

    No team stands behind this project, and it carries no warranty. A reading can be wrong, stale or missing. The source code is open, and a report of a mistake is welcome.

    Privacy

    The site keeps no account, runs no analytics and sets no cookies. It loads no tracking script from any provider. CARTO serves the map tiles, so CARTO sees which tiles your browser requests, and nothing else on this page comes from them. A place you search for goes to the server of this site. That server asks OpenStreetMap where the place is. The request happens only when you select the search row, never as you type. A location you share stays in the browser, and the site sends it nowhere.

    Data sources

    Public Infobanjir JPS Malaysia. Water levels, flood thresholds and rainfall totals. Its reading wins wherever it carries a station. Its own station search also places a pin no other feed can.
    JPS Selangor Infobanjir Selangor stations, and the only source here for cameras, sirens and flood gauges.
    JPS Wilayah Persekutuan (SPHTN) Kuala Lumpur and Putrajaya. Water level and rainfall stations the Selangor feed does not cover, with their coordinates.
    MET Malaysia Weather, not water. Whether rain falls near a station now, and whether more arrives within three hours. The temperature range for the day, and any official warning, come from the same department through data.gov.my.

    Every sensor names the feed its reading came from, under Details. Where two feeds disagree, the newest reading wins. Public Infobanjir is the exception, and its figure wins outright.

    Weather sits beside the water and changes none of it. The rain outlook joins no reading, and it overrides no station. It raises no alert of its own. A warning from MET appears above the alert list, and in the moving strip at the top of the page. It carries the words MET published, and it counts toward no station total. The temperature is a forecast for the day, not a thermometer, which is why the card states today.

    Map symbols

    One pin per place. The glyph states the kind of sensor. The color states the status of that sensor. A kind color is never a traffic light, and a traffic-light color never means anything but status.

    The five kinds: water level, rainfall, siren, flood gauge, camera. Each holds its own color, and none of the five is ever used for status. A river past its alert, warning and danger marks. At danger it fills red, keeps a pulsing halo, and draws over everything else. A red ring marks a river forecast to reach its danger mark within 3 hours. 4 A Monitoring Station: several sensors at one place, counted. If any of them signals, the pin takes that status color instead. Gray and faded: offline, or reporting nothing. It never takes a status color, because a station with no reading must not look confident. 12 Pins too close together to draw apart, with how many. Zoom in, or select the chip, to open them out. A favorite pin draws itself beside the chip and is not in the count.

    Alert criteria

    The On alert panel and the moving strip list a station for one of three reasons. Nothing else on this map raises an alarm.

    HAPPENING NOW A river at or past its danger mark, or a siren sounding. Observed, not predicted. FORECAST A river climbing fast enough to reach its own danger mark within 3 hours. It must climb at least 0.1 m an hour, and its last three readings must not dip. Anything slower is sensor rounding. Anything further out has too little behind it. NOT CURRENT Either of the above, from a station that has since gone quiet. It stays on the list, because a river at its danger mark whose telemetry died is the last thing to hide. It sorts to the bottom, and it counts toward no total.

    JPS publishes every mark for one station. So a small drain and a large river each stand against what floods them. The map draws rainfall, flood gauges and cameras, and none of the three raises an alert. The panel lists a weather warning above the stations, and that warning counts toward no station total.

    Glossary

    alert, warning, danger mark The three levels JPS publishes for one station. Every station holds its own set. rising Forecast to reach its own danger mark within three hours. It is a forecast, not a direction. Monitoring Station A place that carries more than one sensor. Sensors within 50 m of each other are one station, and the map draws one pin for it. Monitoring Node A place that carries one sensor. The card names the kind instead: Water level, Rainfall, Siren, Flood gauge or Camera. stale The station was on alert, and has since gone quiet. offline No reading arrived. The pin goes gray, and it takes no status color. water level The height of the river against a fixed datum. It does not start at zero, which is why a calm station still reads in the thirties. flood gauge The depth of water over a spot that floods. A negative reading is dry ground. intensity class The rainfall bands JPS publishes. They start above 0, 10, 30 and 60 mm in the last hour. frame and tier A frame is one stored camera picture. A tier is how long the archive keeps a frame before it thins the record.

    Expected behavior

    The map is empty and states that it cannot load. This site refuses to draw a map it cannot refresh. During a flood, a water level from an hour ago is worse than none. The reading is twenty minutes old. JPS updates a station about every 25 minutes. Details states when JPS took the reading, not when this site fetched it. The camera picture is not from now. A camera card replays the last three hours. The viewer opens three hours back. Press the live button to return to now. The page did not reload and the figures moved. The map polls every 5 minutes and repaints itself. There is nothing to reload.

          How it is worked out

          Each station scores its reading against its own thresholds, so a small stream at its limit is as hot as a big river at its limit. Rivers and flood gauges both count — whichever is further up its scale wins the spot.

          The color is the band it has crossed: yellow past its alert mark, orange past warning, red at danger. The same three marks the station's own meter draws.

          A flood gauge that has tripped goes straight to red — water standing over a spot known to flood is something observed, not forecast.

          Below the alert mark, nothing is drawn. A map that is warm everywhere says nothing.

          Rainfall has its own layer and scale; sirens and offline stations are not included.

          How it is worked out

          Rain in the last hour, on the intensity classes JPS publishes: light above 0 mm, moderate above 10, heavy above 30, violent above 60.

          The colors are the rainfall pins' own, so a blob and a pin under it agree.

          Every reporting rain gauge counts, and only rain that is actually falling is drawn — a dry gauge paints nothing.

          This is rain, not flooding. It says where the water is arriving, not where it is standing — the water-level heat answers that.
